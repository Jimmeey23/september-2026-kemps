EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Roster changes move attendance — but far less than the timetable does

This report asks the question behind half of studio management: does it matter who teaches the class? Between February and September 2026 Kwality House changed the standing instructor on 29 recurring slots. Each of those changes is a natural experiment — the same day, the same time, the same format, before and after. This report measures what actually happened, controls for everything else that was moving at the same time, and tests whether the instructor-quality measure developed in Report 01 genuinely predicts outcomes.

The answer, in one paragraph

Yes, the instructor matters — measurably, and in a way that can be predicted in advance. Across 29 substitutions the average attendance change, after subtracting what happened in unchanged slots on the same days, was +0.61 attendees per class, with 19 changes positive and 10 negative. More importantly, the size of the change correlates with the difference in the two instructors' schedule-adjusted effects at r = 0.69 — meaning the instructor quality measure built in Report 01 predicted these outcomes before we looked at them. But the effect is modest: the median absolute change was 1.52 attendees per class, against a 6.37 studio average. Roster changes are a real lever, worth roughly a fifth to a third of a class's attendance — not the whole thing. The timetable still dominates.

The five findings that should change how the roster is managed

1 · Roster changes are a real but bounded lever — and the bound is useful
Observation
Changing the standing instructor on a recurring slot moves attendance, but rarely by a lot.
Evidence
Across 29 substitutions the mean net effect was +0.61 attendees per class and the median absolute effect was 1.52. The largest single gain was +4.54 (Wednesday 09:45 Barre 57, Karan Bhatia to Reshma Sharma, July); the largest loss was −4.16 (Wednesday 07:30 Cardio Barre, Anisha Shah to Simonelle De Vitre, April). 19 of 29 changes moved the class by more than one attendee in either direction.
Interpretation
Instructor changes matter at the margin of roughly ±1 to ±2 attendees per class in typical cases, and up to ±4.5 in the extremes. Against a 6.37 studio average that is 15–70% of a class — significant, but nothing like the 2× gaps produced by format and slot choice.
Why it matters
This sets the right expectation. A roster change on its own will not rescue a failing slot; it will shift it by one or two attendees. Format and timing changes are the larger lever.
Recommended action
Bundle roster changes with format or timing changes rather than making them alone. Where a slot needs to move by four or five attendees, instructor substitution alone will not get there.
Expected outcome
More realistic targets and fewer disappointed expectations after a reallocation.
Success metric
Post-change attendance tracked against a pre-committed estimate band of ±1.5 attendees.
2 · Instructor quality predicts outcomes — the Report 01 measure is validated out of sample
Observation
Substitutions toward instructors with higher schedule-adjusted effects produced larger attendance gains.
Evidence
For the 23 substitutions where both instructors have a measured September adjusted effect, the correlation between (new instructor effect − old instructor effect) and the net attendance change is r = 0.69. The largest gains in the entire set were all moves toward instructors with strong effects: Karan Bhatia → Reshma Sharma (+4.54), Karan Bhatia → Pranjali Jain (+3.91), Simran Dutt → Karanvir Bhatia on Sunday 17:15 Mat 57 (+3.62), Cauveri Vikrant → Reshma Sharma on Saturday 08:00 Cardio Barre Express (+3.19).
Interpretation
The adjusted-effect measure built in Report 01 — which was derived only from September data — correctly predicts the direction and rough magnitude of changes that happened between February and September. That is a genuine out-of-sample validation, and it means the measure can be used prospectively.
Why it matters
Management now has a forward-looking tool: before making a substitution, look up the two instructors' adjusted effects and estimate the outcome.
Recommended action
Adopt the adjusted effect as the standing input to every substitution decision, alongside the head-to-head record for that specific slot where one exists.
Expected outcome
Substitutions chosen on evidence rather than on availability.
Success metric
Every substitution decision documented with the two instructors' adjusted effects and an expected delta, reviewed at eight weeks.
3 · "Clients didn't follow the instructor" is the wrong objection — measured properly, most clients never repeat a slot at all
Observation
Client overlap after a substitution is commonly read as damage. It is not.
Evidence
Client overlap averaged 28.8% in the changed slots. But across 511 eight-week windows in recurring slots where the instructor did not change at all, overlap averages 32.8% (median 31.2%), ranging from 15.9% to 73.3% in September alone. And the correlation between overlap and the net attendance outcome is only r = 0.17 — essentially nothing.
Interpretation
Slots rebuild their audiences continuously, whether or not the instructor changes. A 25% overlap is not a 75% loss; it is what a normal eight-week window looks like.
Why it matters
This removes the main argument for leaving weak incumbents in place. The fear that clients will leave is not supported: they turn over anyway, at the same rate, instructor change or not.
Recommended action
Retire client overlap as a decision metric. Replace it with the controlled net-attendance delta, which is what actually matters.
Expected outcome
Faster, better-evidenced substitution decisions.
Success metric
No substitution decision justified by client-overlap figures in future reviews.
4 · The April 2026 reallocation was the single most damaging roster event of the year, and it was never reversed
Observation
Four of the five worst substitutions in the dataset all happened in the same month, on the same day of the week.
Evidence
April 2026: Wednesday 07:30 Cardio Barre (Anisha Shah → Simonelle De Vitre, −4.16), Wednesday 18:15 Cardio Barre Express (Simonelle De Vitre → Karan Bhatia, −3.91), Wednesday 19:15 Barre 57 (Simonelle De Vitre → Karan Bhatia, −2.91), Wednesday 09:00 Barre 57 (Anisha Shah → Simonelle De Vitre, −2.62). Wednesday is now the studio's highest-volume day at 12.2 classes and one of its weakest at 6.83 average.
Interpretation
A single month of Wednesday reallocation removed roughly 13.6 attendees per Wednesday across four slots. Two of those instructors — Karan Bhatia and Simonelle De Vitre — were being moved around each other rather than replaced with stronger options, and one of them exited three months later.
Why it matters
Wednesday carries 61 classes a month. A 3.4-attendee average drag across four slots is roughly 55–60 attendances a month that have never been recovered.
Recommended action
Audit the four April Wednesday reallocations specifically. Three of the four slots are still held by the April incumbents. Where the incumbent's adjusted effect is negative, replace; where it is positive, the problem is the slot, not the person.
Expected outcome
Recovery of the pre-April baseline on those four slots, worth an estimated +30 to +45 attendances per month.
Success metric
Wednesday average attendance above 7.5 within two months, from 6.83 today.
5 · September's changes were, on the whole, well judged — which is worth knowing before anyone reallocates again
Observation
The ten roster changes made in September produced a mean net effect of +1.10.
Evidence
Best: Saturday 08:00 Cardio Barre Express (Cauveri Vikrant → Reshma Sharma, +3.19), Friday 08:30 Barre 57 (Richard D'Costa → Anisha Shah, +1.61), Wednesday 07:30 Strength Lab (Anmol Sharma → Atulan Purohit, +1.65), Saturday 11:30 Strength Lab (Reshma Sharma → Pranjali Jain, +1.57), Wednesday 08:45 Strength Lab (Anmol Sharma → Atulan Purohit, +1.52). Worst: Saturday 11:30 powerCycle (Cauveri Vikrant → Anmol Sharma, −0.35) and Sunday 10:00 Strength Lab (Richard D'Costa → Reshma Sharma, −0.35) — both small.
Interpretation
September's substitutions were directionally correct. The negative cases are explained by the September demand shock (control −1.65), not by the instructors: both were within noise.
Why it matters
The studio has demonstrated it can make good roster decisions. The problem is not decision quality — it is that the underlying allocation of powerCycle slots was never revisited.
Recommended action
Apply the same discipline to the instructor–format pairings identified in Report 03: 426 annual powerCycle classes currently sit with the two lowest-averaging instructors on the format.
Expected outcome
The decision quality already demonstrated in Barre and Strength Lab extended to powerCycle.
Success metric
Mean net effect of the next eight substitutions at or above +0.60.

The roster is shrinking while the schedule holds — and the flexibility cost is invisible in the averages
Observation
Total monthly classes have been essentially flat for a year while the number of instructors delivering them has fallen.
Evidence
Monthly classes ranged from 309 to 370 across the twelve months with no trend. Active instructors fell from 17 (Nov 2025) to 14 (Sep 2026). Richard D'Costa collapsed from 42 classes in October 2025 to 8 in September 2026; Cauveri Vikrant went from 15 to 42 and back to 23.
Interpretation
The studio is concentrating hours onto fewer people without that showing up in the Herfindahl index, because the reduction happened at the bottom of the distribution — the floaters and cover instructors, not the anchors.
Why it matters
Cover capacity is what makes the timetable resilient. Losing it does not show up in any attendance metric until an instructor is unavailable and a class has to be cancelled.
Recommended action
Track active-instructor count and cover capacity explicitly, separately from concentration. Maintain a minimum of four instructors capable of covering any given format.
Expected outcome
Resilience that is visible before it is tested rather than after.
Success metric
Active instructors back to 16+ within a quarter, with at least four cross-trained in each format.
SECTION B

The roster change study

Between February and September 2026, 29 recurring slots changed their standing instructor. For each one we compared the eight classes before the change with the classes after it, then subtracted the change recorded in the same day's slots that did not change instructor. That subtraction is the control: without it, any substitution made during a studio-wide downturn looks catastrophic, and any made during an upturn looks brilliant.

Why the control matters here, more than anywhere else in this series

The raw (uncontrolled) change across these substitutions averages +0.40 attendees per class. The controlled change averages +0.61. The gap is the studio-wide demand movement that had nothing to do with the instructors. In September the control was −1.65 attendees per class — meaning a substitution that "lost" one attendee was actually a gain. Any roster decision taken on raw before-and-after numbers in that month would have been exactly backwards.

SECTION C

The overlap control: why "clients didn't follow" proves nothing

The most common objection to replacing an instructor is that clients came for that instructor and will not come back. This section tests that objection directly by measuring client overlap in recurring slots where nothing changed at all.

Baseline: overlap in unchanged slots

Across 511 eight-week windows in recurring slots where the instructor, day, time and format all stayed the same, client overlap between the first four weeks and the next four weeks averaged 32.8% (median 31.2%). In September alone, unchanged slots ranged from 15.9% to 73.3%.

Client overlap in the 29 slots that did change instructor averaged 28.8% — below the baseline for unchanged slots.

And overlap does not predict outcome

The correlation between client overlap and the net attendance change across all 29 substitutions is r = 0.17. In plain terms: knowing how many clients stayed tells you essentially nothing about whether the slot got better or worse.

The substitution with the highest overlap in the dataset — Wednesday 07:30 Strength Lab, Anmol Sharma to Atulan Purohit, September, 70.6% — produced a net gain of +1.65. The substitution with the lowest overlap — Wednesday 19:15 Barre 57, Simonelle De Vitre to Karan Bhatia, April, 10.0% — produced a net loss of −2.91.

Slots rebuild their audiences continuously — instructor change or not
Observation
Client overlap is low everywhere, changed slots or not.
Evidence
511 unchanged eight-week windows average 32.8% overlap; changed slots average 28.8%. Overlap does not correlate with outcome (r = 0.17).
Interpretation
This is the same finding as Report 03’s habit-formation result, seen from the roster side: attendance at a recurring slot is a continuously rotating pool, so any eight-week comparison will show most faces changing regardless of who is teaching.
Why it matters
The "clients will leave" objection is empirically empty. It is also expensive: it is the reason weak incumbents stay in place.
Recommended action

SECTION E

Exits and their aftermath

Two instructors stopped teaching at Kwality House in July 2026. Neither departure appears in any attendance report, but between them they held recurring slots that had to be reassigned — and the way that reassignment was handled is a useful lesson in how not to do it.

Karan Bhatia — exited July 2026

Taught 11–15 classes a month from October 2025 to June 2026, then zero from July. Across the trailing twelve months he averaged 2.68 on powerCycle over 19 classes — among the weakest measured pairings in the studio.

His slots and what happened to them:

  • Tuesday 09:45 Barre 57 → Reshma Sharma (July): +4.54 net — the single best substitution in the dataset.
  • Wednesday 18:15 Cardio Barre Express → Pranjali Jain (July): +3.91 net — second best.
  • Wednesday 19:15 Barre 57 → Karan Bhatia had taken it over in April from Simonelle De Vitre at −2.91 net, then went to Bret Saldanha / Pranjali Jain.
  • Friday 11:15 Cardio Barre → Anisha Shah (September): +1.01 net.

Read: his departure was, on the evidence, a net positive for the studio — but only because the replacements were deliberately chosen. Two of the four moves rank in the top three of all 29 substitutions studied.

Simran Dutt — exited July 2026

Taught 16–24 classes a month until early 2026, declining to 2 by July, then zero. Held the Sunday evening block.

Her slots and what happened to them:

  • Sunday 17:15 Mat 57 → Karanvir Bhatia (July): +3.62 net — third best substitution in the dataset.
  • Sunday 16:00 Barre 57 → Karanvir Bhatia (July): +1.00 net initially — but the slot averaged 3.25 in September at 16% utilisation, the worst Barre class in the studio.

Read: the Sunday 17:15 Mat 57 reassignment was excellent. The Sunday 16:00 Barre 57 reassignment was not: the substitution effect was positive at the time, but the slot has since collapsed. This is the clearest example in the data of the difference between a good instructor change and a viable slot — Karanvir Bhatia took the class up from where it was and the slot still died underneath him.

An instructor change can be correct and still leave you with a dead slot
Observation
Two Sunday evening slots were reassigned to the same instructor in July 2026 with very different outcomes eight weeks later.
Evidence
Sunday 17:15 Mat 57 → Karanvir Bhatia: net +3.62 at the time, and the slot averaged 9.25 in September at 46% utilisation. Interpretation
The substitution was measured over eight classes. Eight weeks later, one slot had consolidated its gain and the other had lost it. The difference was not the instructor — it was the slot.
Why it matters
An eight-week substitution delta is a measure of the change, not of the destination. Management must review the destination separately.
Recommended action
Add a second review at 8–12 weeks after any substitution, measuring the slot's absolute level (not its delta) against the studio benchmark. A positive substitution into a structurally dead slot still requires the slot to be dealt with.
Expected outcome
Fewer slots that were "successfully" reassigned and then quietly died.
Success metric
Every substituted slot reviewed at 8 weeks on delta and at 16 weeks on absolute level, with both recorded.
Departures should trigger a slot review, not just a replacement search
Observation
When an instructor leaves, the natural response is to find someone to cover their classes. The data suggests a different first question.
Evidence
Of the four slots vacated by Karan Bhatia and Simran Dutt, two became among the studio's best-performing substitutions and one is now its worst-utilising Barre class. The difference was decided by the slot, not by the replacement.
Interpretation
An exit is the cheapest possible opportunity to ask whether the class should exist at all, because no incumbent relationship has to be managed.
Why it matters
Refilling a slot reflexively preserves a timetable decision that may have been wrong years ago.
Recommended action
Adopt a standing rule: when an instructor exits, every slot they held is assessed on its own twelve-month trajectory before a replacement is named. Slots declining faster than −0.15 per month are reformatted rather than refilled.
Expected outcome
Exits become timetable improvement events rather than cover problems.
Success metric
Every vacated slot has a documented trajectory assessment on file before the replacement is confirmed.

CRITICAL

What the headline numbers are not telling us

"The substitution lost two attendees" usually means the studio lost two attendees, not the substitution.
In September the same-day control was −1.65 attendees per class. Saturday 11:30 powerCycle shows a raw change of −2.00 and a net effect of −0.35 — it was essentially neutral in a month when everything fell. Judged raw, a perfectly reasonable decision looks like a failure.
"Only 25% of clients stayed with the slot" is not evidence of damage.
Unchanged slots average 32.8% overlap across 511 eight-week windows. Changed slots average 28.8% — if anything slightly lower, but well within the normal range of 15.9% to 73.3%.
"Karan Bhatia leaving hurt us" is not supported by the data.
His two largest slots were reassigned to Reshma Sharma (+4.54) and Pranjali Jain (+3.91) — the two best substitutions in the entire dataset. He averaged 2.68 on powerCycle over the trailing twelve months. His departure was, on the evidence, a net positive.
"The instructor change worked" can be true while the class still dies.
Sunday 16:00 Barre 57 was reassigned to Karanvir Bhatia in July 2026 at a net +1.00. By September the slot averaged 3.25 at 16% utilisation — the worst Barre class in the studio. The delta was positive; the destination was not.
"Our instructors are interchangeable" and "our instructors are everything" are both wrong.
Substitution effects average +0.61 attendees per class and are predictable from adjusted effects at r = 0.69 — so instructors are not interchangeable. But the median absolute effect of 1.52 against a 6.37 average is a fifth to a third of a class, while format and slot choices move attendance by multiples. Neither extreme is right.
"Concentration is stable, so the roster is healthy" misses the shrinkage.
The Herfindahl index sat between 0.074 and 0.082 every month for a year. Yet the active roster fell from 17 to 14 and two instructors exited. Concentration measures how hours are divided among those who have them; it says nothing about how many people can cover a class next Tuesday.

METHODOLOGY

Methodology, definitions and data limitations

How substitutions were identified and measured

  • Definition: a recurring slot (day + start time + class) whose modal instructor changed between one eight-class window and the next, February to September 2026. 29 identified.
  • Before/after: the eight classes preceding the change and the classes following it, typically eight but fewer where the change happened late in the period.
  • Control: the mean change in attendance over the identical calendar period across all Kwality House slots on the same day of the week whose instructor did not change. This absorbs studio-wide demand movement, festival effects and seasonality.
  • Net effect: raw change minus control. This is the figure used throughout.
  • Overlap: the share of clients attending the slot in the after-period who also attended it in the before-period.

The overlap baseline

  • Built from 511 eight-week windows in recurring slots where instructor, day, time and format were all unchanged, February to September 2026.
  • Mean overlap 32.8%, median 31.2%. Monthly means ranged 33.7% (March) to 28.5% (September); September individual slots ranged 15.9% to 73.3%.
  • Purpose: to establish what "normal" client turnover looks like, so that turnover after a substitution can be judged against something.

Definitions

  • Adjusted effect: from Report 01. Attendees per class versus a roster-average instructor in the identical day, time band, format, capacity and week. Derived from September data only.
  • Teaching load: number of classes taught at Kwality House in the calendar month.
  • Concentration: Herfindahl index of teaching-load shares; effective N = 1 ÷ HHI.
  • Exit: inferred from an instructor's monthly class count falling to zero for three consecutive months. No staffing or HR record was available.

Limitations — read before acting

  • Substitutions are not randomised. Instructors are changed for reasons — illness, departure, performance — and those reasons correlate with outcomes. The same-day control handles studio-wide movement but not slot-specific causes.
  • Small samples per substitution. Eight classes before and eight after is enough to detect a two-attendee change but not a half-attendee one. Effects below about ±1.0 should be treated as noise.
  • The validation correlation uses September-derived effects to predict February–September outcomes. This is out-of-sample in the outcome but not in the instructors, so it validates the measure's usefulness rather than its construction.
  • The control is imperfect in September because the month contained a festival and a closure day; the control absorbs this on average but individual slots may still be affected disproportionately.
  • Exits are inferred, not recorded. It is possible that Karan Bhatia or Simran Dutt are on leave rather than departed; the three-month zero threshold is a judgement, not a fact.
  • No data on instructor cost, contract or availability. Every reallocation recommendation must be checked against commercial and contractual reality before implementation.
