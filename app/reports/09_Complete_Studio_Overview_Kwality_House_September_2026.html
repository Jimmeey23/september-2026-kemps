The studio is not short of demand. It is short of follow-through — on seats, on renewals and on consumption.
Observation
Three independent measures moved the wrong way in September: attendance fell 19.4% month on month, the renewal rate fell to a twelve-month low of 59.8%, and 264 reserved seats were released too late to resell. Against that, cash collected rose 7.0% and revenue per check-in rose 32.7%.
Evidence
Attendance 2,593 → 2,090. Renewal 62.2% → 59.8%. Lost seats 264 (5.7% of 4,606 scheduled). Cash ₹2,996,982 → ₹3,206,431. Revenue per check-in ₹1,156 → ₹1,534. Utilisation 45.4%, meaning 2,516 of 4,606 seats went unused.
Interpretation
These are not contradictory. The studio has become more efficient at extracting value from the visits it gets while the number of visits shrinks. That is a sustainable position for a quarter or two and an unsustainable one beyond it, because the renewal data shows the mechanism: members completing 11 or more sessions renew at 87.0% against 50.9% for those completing one to four (n=2,087). Under-consumption today is next quarter's lapse.
Why it matters
The instinctive response to falling attendance is to sell more. The evidence says the higher-return action is to convert what has already been sold — the 2,516 empty seats, the 264 released late, the 70 lapsed memberships nobody has called, and the memberships already paid for but never used.
Action
Run three workstreams in parallel, in this order of return: seat recovery (60–80 check-ins a month, four weeks to first result), renewal pipeline management (roughly 14 memberships a month retained), and schedule and roster correction (165–195 check-ins a month, eight to twelve weeks).
Expected outcome
Attendance recovering toward the August level of 2,593 without additional marketing spend.
Success metric
Monthly check-ins from 2,090 to 2,315–2,365 within two quarters; renewal rate from 59.8% to 68%; late-cancel rate from 10.7% to below 8%.

The studio in eight numbers

  • 2,090 check-ins across 328 classes in 29 days — 6.37 per class, 72.1 per day.
  • 45.4% utilisation. 2,516 seats went empty. The constraint is not capacity.
  • ₹3,206,431 collected, ₹1,901,973 earned. ₹1,304,458 of entitlement was sold and not yet used.
  • 474 active clients at 4.41 visits each — a little over one visit a week.
  • 59.8% renewal rate on 189 paid memberships expiring. 70 lapsed, worth ₹821,679.

  • 264 seats lost to late cancellation and no-show — 5.7% of everything scheduled.
  • 0% of 208 active memberships on autorenew; only 30 have a card on file to enable it.

Capacity is not the constraint — allocation is
Observation
2,516 of 4,606 September seats went unused, yet 23 classes were full or oversubscribed and Strength Lab ran at 82.4% utilisation. Scarcity and waste are happening in the same month, in the same building.
Evidence
Barre 57: 184 classes, 1,333 check-ins, 40.1% utilisation, 1 full class. Strength Lab: 57 classes, 338 check-ins, 82.4% utilisation, 23 full classes. powerCycle: 87 classes, 419 check-ins, 48.2% utilisation, 3 empty classes. Across the month only 25 of 328 classes were full and 4 were empty.
Interpretation
The schedule is not sized wrongly overall; it is distributed wrongly. Roughly two-thirds of the room goes to a format filling two-fifths of its seats, while the format members actually compete for gets 17.4% of the classes.
Why it matters
Adding classes would make the utilisation number worse, not better. The gain available here comes from moving existing capacity toward the formats that fill, and from fixing the roster on the one format that is declining.
Action
Rebalance the mix before adding supply. Shift capacity from the weakest Barre 57 slots toward Strength Lab, and reallocate powerCycle instruction to the strongest available teachers on that format. Model each move against an unchanged-slot control before making it permanent.
Expected outcome
Higher average attendance without a single additional class on the schedule.
Success metric
Overall utilisation from 45.4% to 51%+; Strength Lab full classes from 23 to under 8 once the waitlist is live.

The studio is earning more per visit from fewer visits — and the gap between those two facts is deferred revenue
Observation
Cash collected rose 7.0% month on month while attendance fell 19.4%. Revenue per check-in rose 32.7%, from ₹1,156 to ₹1,534, and has risen 53% since February (₹1,001).
Evidence
Cash ₹2,996,982 → ₹3,206,431 against check-ins 2,593 → 2,090. Recognised revenue moved far less per check-in: ₹874 → ₹910. The difference between cash collected and revenue recognised widened to ₹1,304,458, the largest in the series.
Interpretation
Part of the improvement is genuine price realisation — the mix shifted from ₹1,942 drop-ins toward annual memberships and private packages. Part of it is money banked against visits that have not happened yet. Only the first part is durable.
Why it matters
The studio has sold ₹1.30m more entitlement than it delivered in a single month. If those members attend, the 45.4% utilisation schedule absorbs them comfortably. If they lapse first, the cash stays and the relationship goes — which is what the renewal rate is already showing.
Action
Track deferred revenue as a balance with an expiry profile, and report consumption alongside collections every month. Treat a rising balance as an obligation, not as performance.
Expected outcome
Revenue quality visible; entitlement expiry prompting contact before it lapses.
Success metric
Deferred-revenue balance and trailing-90-day consumption rate reported monthly; consumption held at or above 70%.

The three retention facts that matter, and the one number that connects them
Observation
Consumption predicts renewal; renewal is falling; and nothing is being done about the members who have already left.
Evidence
Members completing 11+ sessions renew at 87.0%, those completing 1–4 at 50.9% (n=2,087, twelve-month paid cohort). The September cohort of 189 paid memberships renewed at 59.8% with 70 lapses. Of those 70, zero had repurchased a membership or package by 10 October; seven had bought drop-ins or retail worth ₹15,540 in total. The 70 had spent ₹2,756,777 at Kwality House in the preceding thirteen months.
Interpretation
These are the same problem seen at three points in the lifecycle. A member stops attending, does not renew, and is not contacted. The studio then sells them a ₹1,942 drop-in instead of a membership — which is what the seven are doing.
Why it matters
The 70 lapsed members represent roughly ₹2.76m of observed annual spend. Even a 15% win-back returns about ₹414,000 a year. That is more than the entire retail line and it requires no new acquisition.
Action
Own the lifecycle end to end. Consumption trigger at day 21, renewal conversation at day −30 with a named associate, win-back sequence at day +3, +14 and +28 with a named owner. Report all three monthly.
Expected outcome
Renewal rate recovering and lapses returning instead of drifting into drop-in purchasing.
Success metric
Renewal rate 59.8% → 68%; 60-day win-back 0% → 15%; median visits per active member 4.41 → 5.2.

The largest loss and the easiest fix are not the same thing
Observation
Unused scheduled capacity is 2,516 seats — nearly ten times the 264 seats lost to late cancellation and no-show. But the 264 are recoverable in weeks and the 2,516 are not.
Evidence
Late cancellations and no-shows are concentrated: 64.5% of late cancels come from bookings made one to three days ahead, 84% of no-shows come from a single format, and 20% of all lost seats come from ten named clients. Unused capacity is diffuse: 184 Barre 57 classes at 40.1% utilisation, spread across the week.
Interpretation
Concentration is what makes a loss fixable. The 264 lost seats have an identifiable cause, an identifiable moment and, in many cases, an identifiable client. The 2,516 empty seats require changing what is scheduled, which takes a quarter and cannot be done by policy.
Why it matters
Sequencing by size would put the schedule work first and deliver nothing for two quarters. Sequencing by concentration delivers 60 to 80 check-ins a month inside four weeks, which funds the patience the schedule work needs.
Action
Do the concentrated fixes first. Prompts with a holdout, a Strength Lab waitlist, and offender outreach are all live inside four weeks. The schedule and roster programme runs in parallel from week 7.
Expected outcome
Measured gains in month two while the longer programme is still being set up.
Success metric
60+ check-ins recovered within eight weeks, evidenced by the prompt holdout and the waitlist data.

The recovery ceiling, stated carefully

  • Seat recovery (Report 08): +60 to +80 check-ins a month. Three targets overlap — lead-time prompts (35), Strength Lab no-shows (21), repeat offenders (26) — so the combined figure is deliberately conservative against a raw sum of 82.
  • Schedule and roster (Reports 01–05): +165 to +195 check-ins a month. Already modelled with controls in Report 05.
  • Renewal recovery (Report 07): about 14 additional memberships a month at a 68% renewal rate, worth roughly ₹164,000 of membership value monthly before their ongoing consumption.
  • Retail attach (Report 06): about ₹48,500 a month.
  • These are additive. Seat recovery fills seats already sold; schedule and roster work fills seats never sold. They do not compete for the same inventory.

Dependencies that constrain the order

  • Card capture must precede autorenew. Autorenew is impossible for the 178 memberships without a card. This is a hard dependency, not a preference.
  • The prompt holdout must precede rollout. Without it the studio cannot distinguish the intervention from the season.
  • Consumption triggers must precede the renewal push. A renewal conversation with a member who has attended twice is a conversation about a product they did not use.
  • Reconciliation must precede any target setting. Targets set against a number the studio cannot reproduce will be disputed the first month they are missed.

What would change the conclusion

Three things. First, if October and November attendance recovers to 2,400+ without any intervention, the September fall is seasonal and the schedule-and-roster component of the impact model should be halved — the seat-recovery and renewal components are unaffected because they rest on within-month behaviour, not on the trend. Second, if the prompt holdout shows no effect, the largest single item in the seat-recovery case disappears and the target falls from +60–80 to +25–45. Third, if cost data becomes available, the ranking of formats and products by profitability may differ from their ranking by attendance and revenue, and several recommendations here would need revisiting.

Data faults corrected across the programme

  • 565 duplicate rows in the September Bookings slice, on (Member Id, Sale Id, Session Date). Left in, they overstate attendance by 29%.
  • The wrong join key. UniqueID1/UniqueID2 overlap on only 8 of 328 sessions despite appearing in both files. The correct key is Sale Id to SessionID (99.8% match).
  • Double-counted discounting. The sale-level discount column repeats across line items; summing it overstates September by 3.7×. The per-line column is correct.
  • Leaky churn fields. Three fields in the lapsed file are computed as at October 2026, after the outcome they would predict. Excluded from every model.
  • Contradictory status fields. Status and Derived Status disagree on 18,039 of 18,880 records. Status is used throughout and the two are never interchanged.
  • Date contamination. The lapsed file's Amount Paid and Total Sessions Completed columns contain date values; the Sales file uses "-" placeholders in numeric columns. All numeric fields are coerced with errors="coerce".
  • Boolean columns read as strings. Attended, Cancelled, Late Cancelled and No Show return zero when summed without coercion.
  • Day-first dates in the lapsed file require explicit dayfirst parsing or they are silently misread.
  • The Is New field conflates client status with attendance outcome — "Not Attended" is exactly the union of cancelled and no-show records. New-versus-repeat is therefore only defined for attendees, and no report in this programme claims otherwise.

Standing limitations

  • No cost data exists in any source. Instructor rates, rent and product cost are absent. Every figure in every report is in attendance, revenue or value — never margin. This is the largest gap and it should be the first data request.
  • No waitlist data. Refused demand cannot be measured, so the Strength Lab opportunity is a documented floor rather than a ceiling.
  • No room identity. Only capacity is available, so room-allocation recommendations are inferred and must be checked against the actual room schedule.
  • The late-cancel window is undefined in the source. All rates are relative to whatever the studio has configured and cannot be benchmarked externally.
  • October 2026 is partial (1–10 October). Used only for the win-back window and the active-book snapshot, never as a benchmark.
  • Thirteen months is a short series. One September cannot fully separate seasonality from trend; §11 states what would settle it.

A note on confidence labels

High confidence means directly measurable in reconciled data with a stable sample and surviving the controls applied. Moderate means the direction is well supported but the magnitude rests on an assumption stated in the text. Low means the finding depends on behaviour the data cannot observe — these are hypotheses, labelled as such wherever they appear, and no low-confidence item in this programme carries more than a quarter of the total modelled impact.
