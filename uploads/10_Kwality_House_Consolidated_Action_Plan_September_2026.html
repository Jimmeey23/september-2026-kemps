EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

September 2026: the month the growth stopped, and what to do about it

Five reports, one studio, one month. This document consolidates them into a single set of decisions. It states what is known with confidence, what is probable, and what is hypothesis — and it reconciles the places where different chapters appear to disagree.

The situation in four sentences

Kwality House delivered 2,090 check-ins in September 2026 — one per cent fewer than September 2025, ending a twelve-month growth run. The decline is not a festival effect and not a general softening: it is concentrated almost entirely in one format, powerCycle, which lost 148 check-ins year on year while Barre gained 90 and Strength Lab gained 38. Underneath that, the studio has two structural problems that predate September by many months: 31 of its 328 monthly classes run at an average of 2.4 attendees, and its only capacity-constrained product — Strength Lab, full at 82.4% — has not had a class added in a year. And beneath both sits the deepest finding of the series: recurring classes are not building recurring attendance — a median 78.5% of the clients in a weekly class attend it once a month.

What we know, what we believe, what we are guessing

High confidence Established findings

  • September 2026 was flat year on year (2,090 vs 2,110) after twelve months of growth.
  • powerCycle is the entire explanation: −148 check-ins YoY, −11.3pp utilisation; Barre +90, Strength Lab +38.
  • Strength Lab is demand-constrained at 82.4% utilisation; 18 of 57 September classes had bookings above room capacity.
  • 105 of 328 classes (32.0%) ran below 35% utilisation; 2,516 of 4,606 places went unused.
  • New-client 21-day return has collapsed to 9.0%, the worst in twelve months.
  • Only 3 of 14 instructors are statistically distinguishable from the roster average, and all three are negative.
  • Bret Saldanha and Raunak Khemuka taught 426 powerCycle classes in twelve months at a combined 3.70 average.
  • Client turnover in recurring slots is normal, not damage: unchanged slots overlap just 32.8% over eight weeks.

Moderate confidence Well-evidenced inferences

  • The pre-festival softening (1–13 Sep, 6.64 avg) was largely Kwality-specific; the festival dip was city-wide and recovered.
  • Barre is stable in aggregate but its best variants (Mat 57 10.86, FIT 8.80) are under-scheduled relative to Barre 57 (6.43, 69 sessions).
  • Room capacity is mismatched: Barre 57 draws 6.1 in 12-seat rooms and 6.7 in 20–22 seat rooms, producing 51% vs 32% utilisation.
  • Instructor changes are safe but bounded: mean net effect +0.61 attendees per class, median absolute 1.52.
  • The adjusted-effect measure predicts substitution outcomes at r = 0.69 — usable for ranking, not for point forecasts.
  • Three powerCycle slots are among the studio’s most reliable classes (CV 0.14–0.26) and must be protected from any blanket cut.

Low confidence Hypotheses to test, not act on

  • That a commitment or standing-order product would raise habit formation — no evidence in the data that one has been tried.
  • That Thursday 19:15 and Tuesday 17:45 Barre 57 (down 47% and 41% versus trailing) share a common cause — not identified.
  • That Express formats serve a genuine client segment — unknown, and the reason they should not be cut deeply yet.
  • That consolidation to the generic Strength Lab variant has not changed the product — the rename is confirmed, the equivalence is assumed.
  • The full +385 uplift from lifting sub-35% classes to 45% — this is a ceiling, not a forecast.

What this adds up to

Taken together at realistic capture rates — roughly 60% of the quantified upside — these decisions are worth +165 to +195 check-ins per month, taking the studio from 2,090 to approximately 2,260 to 2,285, or about 7.2 to 7.4 per class against September's 6.37 and the pre-festival 1–13 September level of 6.64. That would be a genuine recovery above the underlying trend, not merely a bounce back from a festival month. The single largest component is removing and replacing dead powerCycle supply; the single highest-confidence component is adding Strength Lab capacity, because it is the only change where the studio is currently refusing demand it can document.

RECONCILIATION

Where the reports appear to disagree

Four of the five reports reach conclusions that, read in isolation, contradict each other. In every case the contradiction dissolves once the unit of analysis is specified — but the resolution is not always "both are a bit right", and where one report should win, this section says so.

Report 01: "Instructor effects are mostly noise." versus Report 03: "The instructor allocation is a huge error."
The two positions
Report 01 tests whether individual instructors differ from each other within a single month of 328 classes. They mostly do not — only 3 of 14 are statistically distinguishable, with confidence intervals of roughly ±1.7 attendees. Report 03 looks at 426 powerCycle classes over twelve months by two instructors averaging 3.70 against a peer averaging 8.57 over 145 classes.
Resolution
Both are correct at their own sample size, and the reconciliation is arithmetic. A per-class effect of 2.25 attendees is undetectable in a 20-class sample and overwhelming in a 426-class sample. The right conclusion is: do not use instructor effects to rank one instructor against another on a single month, and do use them to judge volume-weighted allocation over a year. Report 03 wins on allocation; Report 01 wins on individual monthly performance review.
Which report governs
Report 03 (allocation) / Report 01 (individual review)
Report 01: "Rohan Dahima’s #1 ranking is a scheduling artefact." versus Report 03: "Rohan Dahima is the best powerCycle instructor in the studio."
The two positions
Report 01 finds his September adjusted effect (+0.63) is not significant and his raw lead is driven by teaching 40 classes. Report 03 finds he averages 8.57 on powerCycle across 145 trailing-twelve-month classes at 86% utilisation, against a format average of 5.95.
Resolution
Both are right, and together they describe a genuinely valuable instructor whose monthly ranking overstates him. He is the studio’s best powerCycle asset and also carries real key-person risk. Protect him, cap his dispersion, and build understudies — but do not treat his monthly #1 as evidence that the schedule is optimised.
Which report governs
Both
Report 02: "Cut powerCycle." versus Report 03: "Three powerCycle slots are the most reliable classes in the studio."
The two positions
Report 02 recommends reducing powerCycle supply by roughly a third on the basis that the format lost 148 check-ins YoY. Report 03 finds Thursday 18:00 powerCycle (CV 0.14, avg 8.84 over 38 classes), Tuesday 19:15 (CV 0.17, 8.72 over 51) and Wednesday 08:00 (CV 0.26, 8.04 over 52) are the steadiest classes the studio runs.
Resolution
Report 03 wins, and it sharpens Report 02 rather than contradicting it. The cut must be slot-specific, never format-wide. Eight named slots go; five named slots are protected and used as the benchmark. A blanket powerCycle reduction would destroy the three most predictable classes on the timetable.
Which report governs
Report 03
Report 04: "Instructor changes are safe." versus Report 04: "Instructor changes are small."
The two positions
The overlap analysis shows clients are slot-loyal rather than instructor-loyal, so changes carry little demand risk. But the measured net effect of a substitution averages +0.61 with a median absolute of 1.75.
Resolution
Both are true and the distinction matters. Low risk is not the same as high reward. Roster changes are cheap to make and safe to reverse, which makes them a good lever — but they move a class by one or two attendees, not by five. Where a slot needs a five-attendee improvement, the format or the timing has to change too.
Which report governs
Both
Report 02: "Add Strength Lab supply now." versus Report 03: "The roster is not cross-trained enough to support it."
The two positions
Report 02 recommends three additional Strength Lab classes per week on the evidence that the format runs at 82.4% utilisation with 18 of 57 September classes oversubscribed. Report 03 finds effectively only three instructors can teach the format.
Resolution
Report 03 identifies the binding constraint, so sequence follows it. Add the three classes in slots where an existing qualified instructor can take them — Atulan Purohit, Mrigakshi Jaiswal, Anmol Sharma, Pranjali Jain, Vivaran Dhasmana — and run cross-training in parallel for the next phase of expansion. Do not wait for cross-training to add the first three; do not add beyond three until it is done.
Which report governs
Report 03 (sequencing)
Report 03: "Recurring classes have no loyal clients." versus Report 01: "Client return rate is 74.7%."
The two positions
Report 03 finds a median 78.4% one-time share within individual recurring series. Report 01 finds 74.7% of September clients visited Kwality House again in the following 24 days.
Resolution
Both are true and the gap between them is the insight. Clients are loyal to the studio and to their weekly habit; they are not loyal to a specific class. They move between slots on the same morning, or across days, rather than returning to the same recurring class. Any retention strategy built on class-level communities is targeting something the data does not show; one built on studio-level frequency is targeting something it does.
Which report governs
Both
Report 02: "Lift the 105 sub-35% classes to 45% utilisation (+385)." versus Report 03: "Many of those classes have been dead for a year."
The two positions
Report 02 sizes the theoretical capacity prize at +385 check-ins. Report 03 shows the steepest decliners have slopes of −0.82 to −0.29 per month across 8–15 months of history.
Resolution
Report 03 wins decisively. The +385 figure is a ceiling used to size the prize, not a target. Classes with multi-year declines will not recover to 45% through effort. The realistic path is to retire or reformat them and capture the upside by moving demand into formats that already work — which is roughly a third of the headline number.
Which report governs
Report 03

PRIORITY 2

P2 · Controlled experiments — two to eight weeks

Changes where the evidence points in a clear direction but the magnitude is uncertain, or where the outcome depends on client behaviour we cannot observe in the data. Each is structured as a test with a pre-committed success threshold and a stated decision rule if it fails.

Moderate confidence · controlled experiment
Finding
Studio Barre 57 runs 69 sessions at 6.43 average and 37% utilisation. Studio Mat 57 runs 22 sessions at 10.86 and Studio FIT 25 at 8.80. The studio’s most-scheduled class is only its fourth best.
Proposed change
Convert four weekly Studio Barre 57 classes in low-utilisation slots to Mat 57 or FIT: Monday 07:15, Thursday 19:15, Tuesday 17:45 and Sunday 16:00. Hold the instructor and the time constant — change only the format, so the test is clean.
Why
Reallocating format within the same room, instructor pool and client base is the cheapest change available. It requires no new capacity, hiring or marketing.
Expected impact
At 55% capture of the Barre 57 to Mat 57 gap on four weekly classes: +36 to +45 check-ins / month.
KPI
Converted classes average ≥8.5 within six occurrences, and no neighbouring class in the same time slot falls by more than 1.5.
Review period
Six occurrences, then decide. If below 7.0, revert to Barre 57 and treat the slot as a slot problem.
Risk / kill criterion
Format conversion may lose Barre-loyal clients faster than it gains Mat clients. The concurrency analysis suggests only ~3.5% dilution, but that estimate comes from adding classes, not from changing them.
Low confidence · controlled experiment

PRIORITY 3

P3 · Further investigation — eight to twelve weeks

Questions the data raises but cannot answer. Each requires either information the studio does not currently capture, or a period of deliberate observation. None should be acted on yet.

Is the Studio Strength Lab consolidation product-neutral? Investigate — do not act
What we know
Push and Pull variants ran at 16–24 classes a month through July 2026, then fell to 6 and 4 in August and zero from September, while the generic variant rose from 0 to 30 and then 37. The total count is consistent with a relabelling rather than a set of cancellations — but equivalence of content is assumed, not evidenced.
Investigation
Confirm with the programming team that the generic variant covers the same movement patterns, and compare client-level attendance before and after for members who regularly attended Push or Pull specifically.
Why it matters
If the consolidation lost a distinct client segment, some Strength Lab softness would be misattributed to demand rather than to programming.
Do no-shows exist at scale, and where? Investigate — do not act
What we know
Neither data source records members who booked and did not attend, or who cancelled outside the late-cancellation window. Late cancellations are the only visible booking friction, at 10.1% of net bookings — the lowest rate in twelve months, which is more likely to reflect thinner pipelines than better behaviour.
Investigation
Add a no-show flag to the booking system and measure it for one month, split by format, day and time band. A high no-show rate in nominally full classes would change the picture of which classes are actually at capacity.
Why it matters
The Strength Lab capacity case currently rests on 82.4% utilisation of check-ins. If no-shows are material, realisable capacity is lower and the case for adding classes is weaker; if they are not, it is stronger than stated.
How much demand is Strength Lab refusing? Investigate — do not act
What we know
18 of 57 September Strength Lab classes had net bookings above room capacity. There is no waitlist field, so the number of turned-away members is unknown.
Investigation
Install a waitlist on all Strength Lab classes and record entries for four weeks. This converts an inferred opportunity into a measured one.
Why it matters
It is the only opportunity in this plan where supply is the binding constraint, and it is currently sized from a proxy rather than from demand.
Why did the pre-festival softening happen? Investigate — do not act
What we know
Average attendance fell from 7.46 in the 1–13 August baseline to 6.64 in the 1–13 September window — before Ganeshotsav began. Over the same window Bandra fell only 0.16. The decline was therefore largely Kwality-specific.
Investigation

Rules for reading the numbers

Controls that must accompany every read

  • Always compare against same-day unchanged slots. In September the control was −1.65 attendees per class; a change that "lost" one attendee was actually a gain.
  • Use a consistent 21-day retention window. Check-in data ends in late October, so 30-day windows truncate. Every month-on-month retention comparison in this series uses 21 days.
  • Never rank on a single metric. Reports 01, 02 and 03 each show a different "winner" depending on whether the measure is attendance, utilisation or consistency.
  • State the sample size with every average. Several instructors and series have fewer than ten observations; their figures are directional only.

What would falsify this analysis

  • Strength Lab utilisation falling below 70% on the enlarged slot count would invalidate the central capacity case.
  • New-client conversion failing to move after two full months of the second-visit prompt would suggest the problem is the offer or the market, not the process.
  • Repeat share failing to respond to the commitment pilot would indicate that class-level loyalty is not a lever at all, and the P3 habit-formation work should be closed.
  • Attendance continuing to fall after all P1 changes are complete would point to a demand-side cause outside the studio’s control, and the P3 investigation into the pre-festival softening becomes the priority.

METHODOLOGY

Consolidated methodology and limitations

Sources

  • Checkins sheet ("Day End Report - Part 4", gid 0): 151,999 member-level records across all locations; 66,626 at Kwality House; 2,090 September check-ins and 2,325 net bookings.
  • Sessions extract ("Day End Report - Part 3 - Sessions"): 9,782 Kwality House session records, January 2024 to October 2026; 328 in September 2026.
  • Teacher Recurring extract ("Day End Report - Part 3 - Teacher Recurring"): 1,125 Kwality rows of lifetime per-trainer recurring-series aggregates. Header shift identified and corrected; used for corroboration only.
  • Payroll extract ("Day End Report - Part 1 - Payroll"): 1,120 teacher-month rows; 449 at Kwality. Used to cross-check class counts. Contains no cost or rate data.
  • Member extract ("Day End Report - Part 1 - New"): 21,171 member-level rows with lifecycle, retention and visit-frequency fields. The "Month Year" column is inconsistent (61 distinct formats including "Jan-2020" and "January-2020") and was normalised before any cohort use.

Reconciliation

  • Session-level September check-ins (2,090) equal member-level Checked In = TRUE records (2,090) exactly.
  • Bookings (2,325) equal check-ins plus late cancellations (234) in 327 of 328 classes.
  • Four zero-booked September sessions have no member-level records and are retained as genuine empty classes, not treated as missing data.
  • 33 placeholder "Unknown Class" records (June–Aug 2026) excluded; none fall in September.
  • Instructor names were normalised across sources. Karan Bhatia and Karanvir Bhatia are distinct individuals and are treated as such throughout.

Analytical methods

  • Schedule adjustment: OLS of attendance on instructor + day + time band + format + log(capacity) + ISO week, 328 observations, R² 0.318. Effects re-centred on the session-weighted roster mean.
  • Roster change measurement: before/after comparison with a same-day unchanged-slot control; 29 substitutions.
  • Overlap baseline: 511 eight-week windows in slots where nothing changed.
  • Affinity null model: 200 Monte Carlo reshuffles of instructor labels within each client’s own visit history.
  • Trend estimation: OLS slope of monthly average attendance; reported only for series with eight or more months of history.
  • Concentration: Herfindahl index of attendance or teaching-load shares.

Limitations — the five that matter most

  • No cost data. No source file contains instructor rates, room costs or contract terms. Every impact figure is in attendance, not margin. This is the largest gap in the analysis.
  • No-shows are unobservable. Neither source records booked members who did not attend, or cancellations outside the late window. The true no-show rate cannot be calculated.
  • No waitlist data. Refused demand cannot be measured, so the Strength Lab opportunity is a documented floor rather than a ceiling.
  • October 2026 is incomplete. Used only for retention windows, never as a benchmark. The "last three months" comparisons in Report 03 are depressed for every series by the partial month.
  • Room identity is unavailable; only capacity. Room-allocation recommendations are inferred and must be checked against the actual room schedule.
  • Exits are inferred, not recorded. The departure of Karan Bhatia and Simran Dutt is deduced from three consecutive zero months, not from a staffing record.

A note on confidence labels

High confidence means the finding is directly measurable in the reconciled data, has a sample large enough to be stable, and survives the controls applied. Moderate means the direction is well supported but the magnitude depends on an assumption stated in the text. Low means the finding depends on client behaviour or a causal mechanism that the available data cannot observe — these are hypotheses, they are labelled as such wherever they appear, and no low-confidence item in this plan carries more than a quarter of the total modelled impact.
