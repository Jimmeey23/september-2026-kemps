The studio has been measuring the wrong cancellation number
Observation
Late cancellations are 10.7% of the bookings still held at the cut-off — not 10.7% of bookings. Of 1,991 seats held, 214 were released late and 50 were simply not taken.
Evidence
2,549 de-duplicated September bookings: 344 cancelled early (13.5%), 214 cancelled late (8.4%), 50 no-showed (2.0%), 1,941 attended (76.1%). Late-cancel rate on held bookings = 214 ÷ 1,991 = 10.7%. No-show rate on expected arrivals = 50 ÷ 1,991 = 2.5%.
Interpretation
Expressing late cancels as a share of all bookings understates the problem by roughly a fifth, because a third of bookings are cancelled early and never reach the cut-off. Late-cancel rate must be measured against seats held, which is what the front desk can actually act on.
Why it matters
A 10.7% late-cancel rate means roughly one seat in nine that a member was still committed to on the day is released too late to resell. In a month running at 45.4% utilisation, those seats are simply lost.
Action
Adopt held-bookings as the denominator for every cancellation metric, and report late-cancel rate, no-show rate and attendance rate on held bookings weekly by format and by day part.
Expected outcome
A cancellation metric that reflects what the front desk controls.
Success metric
Late-cancel rate reported on held bookings weekly; rate falling from 10.7% to below 8%.

The five numbers that should drive October

  • 10.7% — late-cancel rate on held bookings. 214 seats released too late to resell.
  • 50 no-shows — previously unobservable. 84% of them are Strength Lab bookings.
  • 13.4% vs 8.5% — late-cancel rate for unlimited members against class-package holders. Members with no marginal cost per booking cancel late 58% more often.
  • 22.3% vs 4.0% — late-cancel rate for bookings made one day ahead against bookings made the same day. Booking further out makes late cancellation 5.6× more likely.
  • 402 of 531 — clients with zero late cancellations or no-shows in September (75.7%). The problem is concentrated in a small group.

Observation
Against the session ledger's 2,090 check-ins, the Bookings sheet records 1,941 attended — a shortfall of 149, or 7.1%.
Evidence
279 of 328 sessions (85.1%) reconcile exactly. The entire 149 shortfall sits in the remaining 46 sessions, with a maximum per-session gap of 7. By format: Strength Lab −75 across 57 sessions (−1.3 per session), Barre 57 −37 across 184, powerCycle −37 across 87. Sessions carrying complimentary bookings account for −55 of the total.
Interpretation
This is a coverage gap in a specific slice of the schedule, not a uniform undercount. The Bookings sheet appears to under-record walk-ins, complimentary and staff arrivals, and certain Strength Lab sessions. Late cancellations differ too: the ledger says 234, the Bookings sheet says 214.
Why it matters
Rates calculated from the Bookings sheet — late-cancel rate, no-show rate, attendance rate — are internally consistent and safe to compare with each other. Absolute counts are not. A late-cancel count of 214 should not be quoted as "the studio had 214 late cancels"; the ledger's 234 is the better figure for that.
Action
Use the session ledger for absolute volumes and the Bookings sheet for behavioural rates. Label every figure with its source. Add a monthly reconciliation line to the reporting pack until the two agree.
Expected outcome
No double-counting and no false precision in the reporting pack.
Success metric
Monthly reconciliation variance published and trending toward zero.

Thursday evening is the worst combination in the schedule, and it is a scheduling problem not a member problem
Observation
Thursday carries a 16.0% late-cancel rate, more than double Saturday's 7.5%. Evening slots carry 14.1% against morning's 9.8%.
Evidence
Thursday: 351 bookings, 244 attended, 41 late cancels, 13 no-shows — a combined loss rate of 15.4% of held seats. Saturday: 375 bookings, 305 attended, 23 late cancels, 0 no-shows — 7.5%. Evening: 796 bookings, 86 late cancels, 23 no-shows. Morning: 1,655 bookings, 127 late cancels, 27 no-shows.
Interpretation
The pattern is consistent with a commitment-horizon effect: a Thursday evening booking is made further from the weekend's settled plans, and an evening slot after a workday is the most defeatable commitment in the week. Saturday morning bookings are habitual and are kept.
Why it matters
The studio cannot change when people's weeks are unpredictable, but it can change what it does with the exposure. Thursday evening is where a reminder or a deposit has the most to recover.
Action
Pilot a Thursday-evening intervention. For four weeks, send a push confirmation at 14:00 on Thursday to everyone booked into a Thursday evening class, with a one-tap cancel. Measure the Thursday rate against Wednesday and Friday as controls.
Expected outcome
A measured, causal estimate of what a reminder is worth before rolling it out.
Success metric
Thursday late-cancel rate falling faster than the Wednesday/Friday control; absolute reduction ≥ 3 points.

Unlimited members late-cancel 58% more often than package holders — the expected effect of zero marginal cost
Observation
Bookings made on unlimited products late-cancel at 13.4%. Bookings made on class packages late-cancel at 8.5%. Bookings on single-session and complimentary entitlements late-cancel at 2.0%.
Evidence

Interpretation
A package holder forfeits a finite, paid-for class when they cancel late. An unlimited member forfeits nothing they have paid for at the margin. This is textbook moral hazard and the direction is exactly what the economics predict. The Annual product is the exception at 9.8% — long-tenure members appear to have settled habits.
Why it matters
The studio cannot remove the flexibility that makes unlimited products attractive. But it can price the behaviour: a late-cancel fee or a strike system applied only to unlimited members would change the marginal cost without changing the product.
Action
Pilot a late-cancel strike on unlimited members only. Three late cancels in a rolling 30 days triggers a seven-day booking restriction. Packages are exempt — they already carry a cost. Run for eight weeks and measure against the same weeks last year.
Expected outcome
A measurable reduction in late cancels from the group that generates 82% of them.
Success metric
Unlimited late-cancel rate from 13.4% to below 10%; total late cancels from 214 to under 170.

Booking Lead Time

The strongest behavioural lever in the data, and it costs nothing to pull.

When bookings were made

  • 45.9% of bookings were made on the day of the class.

  • 99.3% were made within a week. Bookings are made very close to the class.

Bookings made one to three days ahead produce 64.5% of all late cancellations
Observation
Late-cancel rate is 4.0% for same-day bookings, jumps to 22.3% for bookings made one day ahead, and stays elevated at 21.5% for two to three days ahead before falling back to 13.2% at four to seven days.
Evidence

Interpretation
A booking made the previous day sits exactly one night of changed plans away from the class. A booking made the same morning is a decision already taken. The four-to-seven-day bucket falls back, probably because those are habitual bookings made well in advance by members with settled routines.
Why it matters
The intervention point is precise and narrow: the 24-to-72-hour window before a class. That is where a confirmation prompt has something to recover, and it is a window the studio currently does nothing with.
Action
Send a confirmation prompt 20 hours before every class to members who booked one to three days ahead — 954 bookings a month, 138 of which will otherwise late-cancel. One tap to confirm, one tap to release. Measure against a randomised holdout of 20%.
Expected outcome
A causal, measured reduction in the 138 late cancels that this window produces.
Success metric
Late-cancel rate in the one-to-three-day window falling at least 4 points against the holdout group.

What It Costs

264 seats, expressed in the units the studio actually manages.

The loss

  • 264 seats lost to late cancellation and no-show in September.
  • 5.7% of the 4,606 seats scheduled in the month.
  • 0.8048780487804879 seats per class across 328 sessions.
  • At the September average of 6.37 check-ins per class, recovering all 264 would lift the monthly average from 6.37 to 7.17 — a 12.6% increase.
  • Utilisation would rise from 45.4% to 51.1%.

The realistic recovery ceiling

  • Full recovery is not achievable — some late cancels are unavoidable.
  • Target 1: lead-time prompts on the 138 late cancels in the 1–3 day window. A 25% reduction recovers 35 seats.
  • Target 2: Strength Lab no-shows, 42 seats. Halving the no-show rate recovers 21 seats.
  • Target 3: repeat offenders, 52 seats in the top 10. Halving recovers 26 seats.
  • These overlap. A conservative combined target is 60 to 80 seats a month — lifting the average from 6.37 to about 6.55–6.61.

How this compares to the other reports

  • Report 01 (Instructor Performance) and Report 04 (Roster Impact) together identify +165 to +195 check-ins a month from schedule and roster change.
  • This report identifies +60 to +80 check-ins a month from recovery of seats already sold.
  • They are additive, not competing: one fills empty seats, the other stops full seats going empty. Combined, the target range becomes +225 to +275 a month, lifting the average from 6.37 to roughly 7.06–7.21.
  • Recovery is the cheaper of the two. It requires no schedule change, no hiring and no marketing spend — only prompts, a waitlist and a policy.

Recommendations

Six actions, sequenced. Each states the arithmetic and the measure that will tell you it worked.

A1. Fix the denominator before anything else

P1 · This week · Owner: Studio manager

Late cancels are 10.7% of held bookings, not 10.7% of bookings. Reporting on all bookings understates the problem by a fifth. Adopt held bookings as the denominator for late-cancel, no-show and attendance rates, and publish the three together weekly by format and day part.

Execution: A weekly cancellation dashboard on the held-bookings denominator.

Success metric: Late-cancel rate reported correctly every week; baseline set at 10.7%.

A2. Send a confirmation prompt 20 hours before class

P1 · Three weeks · Owner: Front-desk leads + app admin

The 1–3 day-ahead window holds 954 bookings and produces 138 of 214 late cancels (64.5%), at a 21.5–22.3% rate against 4.0% for same-day bookings. Prompt everyone in that window with one tap to confirm and one to release. Hold back 20% as a control.

Execution: Automated prompt with a randomised holdout so the effect is measured, not assumed.

Success metric: Late-cancel rate in the 1–3 day window falling ≥ 4 points against the holdout.

A3. Waitlist and auto-release for Strength Lab only

P1 · Four weeks · Owner: Studio manager

Strength Lab and Full Body are 17.4% of bookings but 84% of no-shows (42 of 50), at 13.6% and 14.9% no-show rates. The format ran at 82.4% utilisation with 18 of 57 classes oversubscribed. Overbook to 110% with a waitlist and auto-release unconfirmed bookings two hours out.

Execution: Waitlist live for Strength Lab; auto-release scheduled; confirmation required on booking.

Methodology & Limitations

De-duplication, definitions, and three data faults that had to be corrected before any figure could be trusted.

Sources and scope

  • Primary: Bookings sheet, "Bookings Data 2018–2026" — 215,227 rows, 29 columns, session dates from 1 Jan 2020 to 17 Oct 2026. 84,322 rows for Kwality House, Kemps Corner.
  • September slice: 3,114 raw Kwality House rows dated 1–30 September 2026.
  • Comparison sources: Day End Report session ledger (328 September sessions) and the checkins extract (2,325 September rows).
  • Studio: Kwality House, Kemps Corner only, on Location Name.

Three data faults that were corrected

  • 565 duplicate rows. The September slice contains repeated records on (Member Id, Sale Id, Session Date). De-duplicating 3,114 rows to 2,549 cut the attended count from a false 2,506 to the correct 1,941 — a 29% overstatement if left in.
  • The join key is Sale Id to SessionID, not UniqueID1/UniqueID2. Despite both files carrying the UniqueID pair, only 8 of 328 sessions overlap on it. Sale Id matches 99.8% of September sessions.
  • Sale Id is not unique to a session over time. One Sale Id (142622709) carries 172 rows. De-duplication before per-session aggregation is mandatory.
  • Attended, Cancelled, Late Cancelled and No Show read as strings in the raw export and must be coerced to boolean before summing; summing them directly returns zero.

Definitions

  • Held bookings — bookings not cancelled early. This is the denominator for every behavioural rate.
  • Late-cancel rate — late cancellations ÷ held bookings. No-show rate — no-shows ÷ held bookings. Attendance rate — attended ÷ held bookings.
  • Lead time — whole days between the booking date and the session date. Same-day is 0.
  • Lost seats — late cancellations plus no-shows. Early cancellations are excluded: the seat returns to inventory.

Limitations — the five that matter most

  • The late-cancel window is not stated in any source. The Bookings sheet carries a Late Cancelled flag but no definition of the cut-off. All rates here are relative to whatever the studio has configured, and cannot be compared to an external benchmark.
  • The Is New field conflates client status with attendance outcome. "Not Attended" is exactly the union of cancelled and no-show records, so new-versus-repeat is only defined for people who attended. It is therefore not possible to say whether new clients late-cancel more or less than repeat clients — the data cannot answer that question, and this report does not claim an answer.
  • 149 September arrivals are missing from the Bookings sheet. Rates are internally consistent; absolute counts are understated. See §3.
  • Coverage is uneven in early September. 1–8 September runs 62.5–100%. Day-level counts in that window should not be quoted.
  • No waitlist data exists. It is not possible to measure how many members wanted a released seat and could not get one, so the recovery figures here are a floor on lost demand, not a ceiling on recoverable demand.

A note on confidence labels

High confidence means directly measurable in reconciled data with a stable sample. Moderate means the direction is well supported but the magnitude rests on an assumption stated in the text. Low means the finding depends on behaviour the data cannot observe. The one question this report cannot answer — whether new clients cancel more than repeat clients — is stated as unanswered rather than guessed at.
