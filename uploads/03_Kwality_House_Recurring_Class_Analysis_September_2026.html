EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The recurring schedule is not building habits

A recurring class is the studio's basic promise: the same class, the same time, the same week. Clients build their week around it, and the studio builds its revenue around it. This report examines all 76 recurring series that ran at Kwality House in September 2026 against their own histories, and asks three questions: which ones are growing, which are dying, and are any of them actually creating the repeat behaviour a recurring timetable exists to create?

The finding that reframes the recurring schedule

Across the 76 recurring series with enough clients to measure, a median of 78.5% of the people who attended a recurring class in September attended it exactly once that month. Only 15.3% of them came to half or more of the occurrences. Five series had not a single client attend half their September sessions. In other words the schedule is recurring but the attendance is not: these are weekly classes being consumed as drop-ins. Everything else in this report — the declines, the volatility, the instructor effects — sits downstream of that one fact.

The five findings that should change the recurring schedule

1 · Recurring classes are being consumed as drop-ins, not as commitments
Observation
Clients who attend a recurring class mostly attend it once.
Evidence
Across 76 September series with at least eight unique clients, the median share of clients attending only once was 78.5%. The median share attending half or more of the month's occurrences was 15.3%. Friday 19:15 powerCycle Express had 14 unique clients and not one of them came twice. Thursday 19:15 Barre 57 had 22 unique clients and a 4% repeat rate — despite trailing-12-month average attendance of 8.68.
Interpretation
Attendance in recurring classes is being generated by a rotating pool of occasional clients rather than by a committed cohort. The schedule provides the supply; it does not create the habit.
Why it matters
A recurring class with a committed cohort is predictable revenue with low acquisition cost. A recurring class supplied by one-off drop-ins is indistinguishable from a walk-in class, and it is far more expensive to fill because it must be re-sold every week.
Recommended action
Stop measuring recurring classes on headcount alone. Add two metrics to the monthly pack for every recurring series: repeat-client share and the count of clients attending ≥50% of occurrences. Set a floor of 25% repeat share; anything below it gets redesigned rather than protected.
Expected outcome
A cohort-based view of which recurring classes are real and which are nominal, within one month of reporting.
Success metric
Studio-wide median repeat share rises from 21.6% to 30% within three months.
2 · The steepest declines are in series nobody is watching, and they are months old
Observation
The most steeply declining recurring series at Kwality House has been falling for at least eight months.
Evidence
Thursday 09:30 powerCycle: slope −0.82 attendees per month across 8 months of history, from a peak of 9.0 (Sept 2025) to 1.67 in the last three months. Wednesday 09:00 Barre 57: −0.52 across 10 months, peak 11.0 (Dec 2025) to 5.45. Sunday 11:30 powerCycle: −0.41 across 14 months. Thursday 10:15 Back Body Blaze: −0.29 across 9 months.
Interpretation
These are not September problems. They are multi-month trajectories that the September snapshot makes look like a one-month dip.
Why it matters
A series declining 0.8 per month will hit zero within a year. Every month of delay costs roughly the difference between its current average and the replacement class average.
Recommended action
Institute a monthly "decline watch" on every series with eight or more months of history and a slope worse than −0.15 per month. Today that is 12 series. Each requires a documented decision: redesign, re-time, re-instructor or retire.
Expected outcome
Decisions taken while a series still has a recoverable base rather than after it reaches zero.
Success metric
No recurring series with eight-plus months of history has a slope worse than −0.15 for two consecutive quarters without a documented intervention.
3 · The most reliable recurring classes in the studio are powerCycle — in the five slots that work
Observation
Reliability and weakness are not the same thing, and conflating them would destroy good classes.
Evidence
Thursday 18:00 powerCycle: 38 classes over twelve months, average 8.84, coefficient of variation 0.14 — the most predictable class the studio runs. Tuesday 19:15 powerCycle: 51 classes, 8.72 average, CV 0.17. Wednesday 08:00 powerCycle: 52 classes, 8.04, CV 0.26. All three run at 80–88% utilisation. By contrast the least reliable series are Saturday 17:00 Strength Lab (CV 1.05, average 1.93) and Monday 18:00 powerCycle (CV 0.97, average 2.43).
Interpretation
powerCycle does not have a format problem. It has a slot problem — and the failing slots have been failing for years while the good slots have quietly been the studio's most dependable classes.
Why it matters
A uniform "cut powerCycle" instruction would remove the three most reliable classes on the timetable. The correct cut is slot-specific.
Recommended action
Protect Thursday 18:00, Tuesday 19:15 and Wednesday 08:00 powerCycle explicitly, and use them as the benchmark against which every other powerCycle slot is judged.
Expected outcome
The studio retains roughly 26 classes a month at 8.0–8.8 average while removing the 31 classes at 2.4.
Success metric
All three protected series maintain CV below 0.30 and average above 7.5 for the next two quarters.
4 · Two instructors have taught 426 powerCycle classes in twelve months at an average of 3.7
Observation
The recurring schedule has locked a large block of powerCycle supply to the two weakest measured instructors.
Evidence
Over the trailing twelve months, Bret Saldanha taught 262 powerCycle classes averaging 3.96 (39% utilisation) and Raunak Khemuka taught 164 averaging 3.39 (34%). Together that is 426 classes — 26.5% of all Kwality House classes in the period — producing 1,650 check-ins. Rohan Dahima, by comparison, taught 145 powerCycle classes averaging 8.57 at 86% utilisation. Karan Bhatia, who exited in July 2026, taught 19 at 2.68.
Interpretation
This is the single largest allocation error visible anywhere in the data. It is not a coaching problem at this scale — 426 classes is two and a half years of weekly classes — it is a scheduling decision that has been left unexamined.
Why it matters
If those 426 classes had been taught at the studio's powerCycle average of 5.95 they would have produced 2,534 check-ins rather than 1,650 — a difference of 884 attendances, or roughly 74 a month.
Recommended action
Do not renew the recurring powerCycle assignments held by Bret Saldanha and Raunak Khemuka. Reallocate them to Rohan Dahima, Vivaran Dhasmana, Anmol Sharma and Cauveri Vikrant, who average 7.65, 8.00, 6.10 and 6.00 respectively on the format. Retain Bret only on Tuesday 10:00 powerCycle (5.40, +0.40 versus its trailing average), which is his one slot performing above benchmark.
Expected outcome
Moving 400+ annual powerCycle classes from a 3.7 average toward a 6.0 average is worth an estimated +55 to +75 check-ins per month at realistic capture rates.
Success metric
powerCycle instructor-weighted average above 6.0 within two months; Bret and Raunak below 15 combined powerCycle classes per month.
5 · The strongest recurring classes are capacity-capped and have been for a year
Observation
The studio's best recurring series are not under-performing; they are full, and have been full for months.
Evidence
Saturday 10:15 Mat 57: 51 classes in twelve months, 14.41 average, 74% utilisation on a 19.6-seat room — and 123 sessions lifetime according to the recurring-series extract, with a lifetime fill rate of 88.3% and only one empty session ever. Tuesday 08:30 Strength Lab: 129 lifetime sessions, 1,229 check-ins, 65.0% fill. Friday 09:00 FIT: 110 lifetime sessions, 1,121 check-ins, 83.0% fill. Monday 08:30 Mat 57: 117 lifetime sessions, 1,330 check-ins, 57.5% fill.
Interpretation
These series have demonstrated sustained demand for over a year and the studio has not added a single additional occurrence.
Why it matters
Growth for this studio is not primarily a marketing problem. Four recurring series have been turning clients away or filling to near-capacity for twelve months while 31 classes a month run at 2.4 average.
Recommended action
Add a second occurrence in the same week for the two most saturated series — a Saturday 10:15 Strength Lab and a Sunday 10:15 Strength Lab (see Report 02) — and install a waitlist on Saturday 10:15 Mat 57 immediately.
Expected outcome
+22 to +30 check-ins per month from the additions, plus visibility on the refused demand that is currently invisible.
Success metric
A recorded waitlist on Saturday 10:15 Mat 57 within four weeks; the new classes averaging ≥6.0 within six occurrences.

SECTION A

Recurring class inventory and longitudinal performance

A recurring series is defined here as a day + start time + format family. Format families consolidate the four Strength Lab variants (generic, Full Body, Pull, Push) and Amped Up! into one series, because the studio retired Push and Pull during August and September 2026; treating them separately would manufacture a false "series churn" signal. See the methodology section for the evidence.

Why Strength Lab variants are consolidated

Classes per month by Strength Lab variant, February to October 2026. Push and Pull ran consistently at 16–24 classes per month through July 2026, then collapsed to 6 and 4 in August and zero from September, while the generic "Studio Strength Lab" variant rose from 0 to 30 and then 37. This is a relabelling, not a set of new or cancelled series. Analysing the variants separately would show four series ending and one starting, none of which happened.

CRITICAL

What the headline numbers are not telling us

"85 recurring classes" overstates how much of the schedule is genuinely recurring.
Of 299 recurring series ever run at Kwality House, the median run length is four months and 43.1% lasted three months or less. A good part of what the studio calls its recurring schedule is a series of short experiments that were never formally closed out.
"The recurring schedule is stable" is contradicted by the trend lines.
76 series have eight or more months of history; 31 of them are declining. The median slope is +0.05, which sounds flat, but it is the midpoint of a bimodal distribution — a cluster of improving series and a tail of steeply declining ones, not a stable set.
"Average attendance" hides whether a class has any regulars at all.
Thursday 19:15 Barre 57 has a trailing-twelve-month average of 8.68 — respectable — and a September repeat rate of 4%. Its 8.68 is produced afresh every week by different people. A monthly average cannot distinguish a community from a queue.
"powerCycle is failing" is wrong; "eight powerCycle slots are failing" is right.
Thursday 18:00 powerCycle averages 8.84 over 38 classes with a CV of 0.14 — the steadiest class in the studio. Tuesday 19:15 averages 8.72 over 51. Wednesday 08:00 averages 8.04 over 52. These three are among the studio's best assets and they are in the format most often written off.
"Cut the weakest classes" would remove the studio's most reliable inventory if applied on average attendance alone.

"Our recurring classes have loyal clients" is not supported by the booking data.
Median repeat share within a single month is 21.6%. Five series had zero clients attend half of their September occurrences. The loyalty the studio perceives may exist at the studio level — 474 clients making 2,090 visits — but it is not attached to specific recurring classes.

Methodology, definitions and data limitations

Sources

  • Sessions extract ("Day End Report - Part 3 - Sessions"): 9,782 Kwality session records, January 2024 to October 2026, used for all series-level, longitudinal and instructor-format measures.

  • Payroll extract ("Day End Report - Part 1 - Payroll"): teacher-month rollups used to cross-check class counts per instructor.

Data-quality note on the Teacher Recurring file

This file's column headers are shifted one position to the left relative to their values. The value appearing under Classes is the total session count; under TotalSessions is the empty-session count; and so on through FillRate, which actually holds revenue per session. This was verified arithmetically: for Saturday 11:30 Strength Lab the file shows 6 sessions, 1 empty, 5 non-empty, 34 checked in, 42 capacity, and a revenue-per-session figure of 6,387.30 — and 34 ÷ 42 = 80.95%, 34 ÷ 6 = 5.67 and 38,323.81 ÷ 6 = 6,387.30 all reconcile exactly under the shifted reading.

Consequence: this file is used in this report only for corroborating lifetime session counts and fill rates on the longest-running series, and every figure taken from it has been checked against the shifted interpretation. It has not been used for any trend, slope or instructor-effect calculation.

Definitions

  • Recurring series: day + start time + format family. 85 ran in September 2026.
  • Format family: the four Strength Lab variants plus Amped Up! are collapsed to one family because the studio retired Push and Pull during August–September 2026. Without this collapse, four series appear to end and one to begin when in fact nothing changed.
  • Slope: ordinary least squares fit of monthly average attendance on month index, in attendees per month. Reported only for series with eight or more months of history.
  • CV: standard deviation ÷ mean of attendance across sessions, trailing twelve months.
  • Repeat share: share of a series' September unique clients who attended it two or more times that month.
  • ≥50%-occurrence share: share of a series' September unique clients who attended at least half of that series' September occurrences.
  • Top-5 concentration: share of a series' September visits made by its five heaviest clients.

Limitations — read before acting

  • Habit formation is measured within a single month. A client who attends a weekly class every other week registers as a repeat client but not a half-attender. The 21.6% repeat figure is therefore a floor on repeat behaviour, not a complete measure of loyalty.
  • The last-three-months comparison includes a partial October 2026. October is incomplete in the source data, which depresses every "last 3m" figure. Use the September column for reliable comparisons.
  • Series continuity is inferred from the timetable, not from a schedule-change log. The studio does not record why a series started or stopped, so "churn" cannot be separated from deliberate experimentation.
  • Capacity is inferred from session records, not from a room register. Room-identity conclusions are indirect.
  • Opportunity 4 (habit formation) is the most speculative estimate in this report. It assumes a commitment product changes behaviour; there is no evidence in the data that such a product has been tried, and the estimate of +60 to +125 should be treated as a hypothesis to be tested, not a target.
  • Instructor–format pairings with fewer than eight classes are excluded. Several instructors therefore appear in only one or two cells of the specialisation map, which understates their true range.
